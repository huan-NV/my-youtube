Hello, the story I’m going to tell you today took place on March 20, 1945, a Tuesday morning at 7 a.m., in an alley on Xin Xue Road in Shanghai, China. The daily rhythm of life gradually woke each household; wooden doors creaked open and closed in succession, carrying the simple breath of urban life. That alley was Jiangyuan Alley.
Its name came from Zhang Zhenxing Jiangyuan, a well-known figure of the time, and it lay near Jiaolou, Xinchalu, and Huanghe Road. The alley ran east–west and was less than 150 meters long. On both sides stood dozens of dilapidated houses lined closely together.
Although the alley itself was not very wide, it was home to hundreds of households from all walks of life—traders, apprentices, dancing girls, and so on. In the mornings, the alley entrance was busier than ever, with all kinds of snack stalls and dim sum shops, and the hawkers’ cold shouts never ceased. Because it had been built long ago, the alleyway was paved with unevenly laid stones. Whenever it rained, water pooled everywhere, turning the road into a muddy mess.
Worse still, when the night-soil cart passed by, some residents would even dump their chamber pots into the street along with the rainwater. At those times, the alley was no different from a public latrine, reeking with an unbearable stench. Because of this, Jiangyuan Alley was regarded by outsiders as filthy and shabby.
At No. 85 near the end of the alley, the ground floor housed a fortune-telling shop called Wang Tayang. The proprietor, Wang Tayang, made a living reading faces by day. But he was not the owner of the house; he was a tenant.
He used only the ground floor for business and subleased the upstairs. That morning, when he and his wife woke up to wash, they happened to see a streak of red liquid on the stair handrail. Looking upward, they discovered that the liquid was dripping from the floor of the upstairs courtyard.
The couple tore off a piece of white paper to wipe it, and only then were horrified to realize it was not water at all but blood. Startled, Wang Tayang froze for a moment, then hurried upstairs to knock on the door.
Five minutes later, the door slowly opened. As soon as Wang Tayang stepped inside, a chill ran straight up his spine. The room was mottled with blood everywhere.
The resident there, Mrs. Zhang, was standing with both hands covered in blood. Her expression was dazed; she made no attempt to avoid anything, as if she were completely lost in her own world. A bad premonition sprang up in Wang Tayang’s heart.
He walked over to the wardrobe most stained with blood, intending to force it open to have a look. At that moment, Mrs. Zhang’s expression suddenly changed, and she rushed to stop him—but it was already too late.
When the wardrobe was opened, a bloodied head appeared before his eyes. Wang Tayang felt dizzy; terror washed over his face, and his body swayed, unable to stand firm. Next to him, Mrs. Zhang spoke in a slow, hoarse voice.
Her voice was so rough it was as if her throat had been abraded with sandpaper. She said she had killed “Da Kuitou.” Da Kuitou was her husband, Zhang Wenying. He was 30 years old, from Anhui, 1.85 meters tall, and weighed 100 kilograms.
His build and strength earned him that nickname. He was known as a strong porter and had once run errands at a nearby pawnshop. Later, he opened a second-hand shop selling used clothes and other items.
His temperament, however, was the opposite of his given name “Wenying,” which implies refined and handsome. He was a wastrel—rude and volatile. As for his wife, Mrs. Zhang, she was 29 years old, originally from Danyang, Jiangsu. Her maiden surname was Du; she was an orphan. After being adopted by the Zhu family to serve as a maid, her surname was changed to Zhu; her full name was Zhu Chunlan.
The Zhu family did not treat her badly; they never showed contempt and even helped arrange a good match for her. The marriage between Mrs. Zhang and Zhang Wenying was put together by the Zhu family. At that time, Zhang Wenying saw that she was good-looking, soft-spoken, and close in age, so he agreed readily. As for Mrs. Zhang, a maid who ate others’ food and lived under their roof, she had no right to refuse and could only passively accept.
Thus she married in a muddled way and took her husband’s surname, becoming Mrs. Zhang when she was just 21.

In the eyes of outsiders, the couple couldn’t be said to be deeply in love, but it was not to the point that she would kill her husband. About an hour before the discovery, around 6 a.m., while half-awake, Wang Tayang had vaguely heard shouting and thuds upstairs.
He thought it was just a domestic quarrel and reflexively went up to ask what was going on. Mrs. Zhang did not open the door; she vaguely replied through it that Zhang Wenying was drunk and raving. Though he found it odd, Wang felt it inappropriate to pry and went back downstairs to sleep.
Who would have thought such a horror would follow? Still reeling, Wang hurriedly told his wife to be cautious with Mrs. Zhang, then immediately went to the nearby police station to report it.
He was so tense because he feared being implicated. In Shanghai at that time, residential neighborhoods had mutual guarantee and supervision to control unlawful behavior. If a violation was discovered, it had to be reported immediately, or one could be held accountable.
Police arrived promptly upon receiving the report, sealed off both ends of Jiangyuan Alley, and began investigating. The room where the Zhangs lived was dark and filled with a chilling atmosphere; blood was on the bed, the floor, even in the washbasin. It was clearly a horrifying crime scene.
The body of Zhang Wenying had been dismembered into 16 pieces and placed in a large wooden trunk. The victim’s throat, both arms, fingers, and abdomen all bore numerous cuts. When Mrs. Zhang was escorted downstairs, she wore only thin clothing and spoke in a trembling voice.
She said to the police, “Please help me.” At first, the officers thought she was begging for leniency for her crime. But when they saw how frail she was, they understood.
They gave her a coat to wear so she could retain some modesty. On the way out, large crowds of nearby residents gathered to gawk.
One minor among them, interviewed decades later, still remembered the scene vividly. Driven by intense curiosity, the boy squeezed his way to the front to watch, only to see the trunk containing the dismembered body had not yet been removed.
The sight delivered a great shock to his young mind, and he could not help but cry out. How could Mrs. Zhang be so cruel—so vicious toward her own husband? At the police station, Mrs. Zhang remained calm and frankly admitted to killing her husband. When asked why, she said, “He treated me too harshly, too cruelly. I hated him, so I had to kill him.”
Mrs. Zhang also insisted she acted alone and that no one else was involved. The police did not believe her, because the victim was tall and strong, while Mrs. Zhang was petite, about 1.5 meters tall and weighing only 40 kilograms.
Such a disparity in size made committing the crime far more difficult. With that in mind, the police formed a new hypothesis: Mrs. Zhang might have had a lover who helped her.
Meanwhile, the crime scene was soon surrounded by reporters who had heard the news; it was packed to the point water couldn’t pass through. Although the house was sealed off, they still tried to extract information from neighbors and probe the details. Their suspicions matched the police: they leaned toward the theory that Mrs. Zhang and a lover committed the crime.
To attract attention, some reports embellished and moralized her “improper conduct,” lambasting her as an unchaste wife and shaping her image as a venomous woman. To heighten drama, they even fabricated that the informant, Wang Tayang, was blind and had discovered the blood because it dripped directly onto his face. For a time, the case became a sensational topic across Shanghai.
After reading the papers, people all criticized Mrs. Zhang’s conduct. Rumors spread that she would be paraded publicly for humiliation, and even the route of the procession was drawn up with apparent authenticity. The rumor grew so widespread that it choked off traffic along related routes; crowds gathered, many craning their necks to see Mrs. Zhang’s face.
Only after the police publicly refuted the rumor did it subside. But was the truth really as the media and police believed? From a modern perspective, one can easily spot inconsistencies. However, in that era of scarce information, even if Mrs. Zhang had a thousand things to say, she could not vindicate herself.
In the first year after marriage, her husband had not fully revealed his true nature. He worked carefully and spoke somewhat gently. This gave Mrs. Zhang, who had known loneliness and hardship since childhood, a long-missing warmth and sense of support.

But not long after, under the influence of bad friends, Zhang Wenying fell into gambling and often stayed out all night. With the bankruptcy of his second-hand shop, he became more violent. All household income fell entirely under his control.
He often sought pleasure outside, leading a dissipated life. The times he brought home money in a month could be counted on one hand. Beating his wife became commonplace—even in public he did not restrain himself.
What angered Mrs. Zhang even more was her husband’s debauchery. He even had an affair with a maid to the point that she became pregnant. When the pregnant maid was kicked out by her employer, she came to Zhang Wenying for help.
She was eight months pregnant, close to delivery, yet Zhang brazenly demanded that Mrs. Zhang handle this “mess” for him. With no choice, Mrs. Zhang took the maid to the hospital and cared for her during childbirth. Afterwards, she did her best to find a good family to take the child in, temporarily patching over the scandal.
She had cried and she had pleaded, but her husband remained indifferent. Bound by traditional notions, Mrs. Zhang did not dare—and had never thought—to seek a divorce. To survive, she could only earn money herself.
She tried to find work outside, even accepting a neighbor’s introduction to a nearby tobacco factory. Every day she left at dawn and returned late at night. Life was hard, but at least she could eat her fill.
Yet in that era, the traditional concept that men worked outside and women stayed home was deeply rooted. Women who worked outside were often seen as a sign of family disgrace. When Zhang learned of it, he felt humiliated, flew into a rage, and maliciously suspected his wife of having a lover.
This thought made him determined not to let her go out to work—at any cost. Meanwhile, he still clung to all the household income. After one drunken episode, he beat her so badly she was bedridden for days.
With no choice, Mrs. Zhang quit her job and began selling or pawning valuables in the house to cover daily expenses. She went everywhere borrowing from friends and neighbors, but after asking too many times, no one would lend to her. In the end, she survived by running tabs at second-hand shops, bun stalls, and even breakfast eateries.
Everywhere—pawnshops, bun stalls, breakfast counters—there were ledgers recording her debts. The prolonged physical abuse and mental pressure worsened her psychological state to the point she often contemplated suicide. One day in October 1944, after failing to borrow money from a second-hand shop, she stole a bottle of disinfectant, went home, and resolutely attempted to drink it.
Fortunately, neighbors quickly noticed something was wrong and rushed her to the hospital in time, saving her life. Seeing her misery, the neighbors were filled with pity.
They once raised money, hoping to help her open a small stall, but unexpectedly the goodwill funds ended up in Zhang Wenying’s pocket. Unable to find a way out, Mrs. Zhang conceived another plan. She considered setting up a small stationery stand at the gate of Jiangyuan Primary School.
She also noticed many small food stalls gathered at the mouth of Jiangyuan Alley and thought about selling pork chops with French fries, a dish she made well. After some thought, she decided to open a stall selling those pork chop and potato fries. The idea filled her with hope like never before.

Happily, she pawned her red cheongsam and used the money to buy a vegetable cleaver in preparation for chopping ribs later. At 3 a.m. on March 20, Zhang Wenying staggered home drunk in a seemingly good mood. Mrs. Zhang told him all her plans in detail.
Unexpectedly, after hearing her out, he called it absurd, not only cursing her viciously but loudly threatening that even if she did make money, he would gamble it all away. After that, he snored and fell back asleep. Seeing every path to a livelihood blocked, anger rose in Mrs. Zhang’s heart beyond control.
At that moment, a voice in her head told her to kill, promising that after killing, she would be redeemed. After three hours of fierce inner struggle, at 6 a.m. she finally took the newly bought cleaver and brought it down on the sleeping Zhang. After killing her husband, not knowing how to handle the body, she crudely imitated scenes from operas, dismembered him into 16 pieces, and put them into a wooden trunk to destroy the evidence.
That trunk had been used for the couple’s clothes, but because of Zhang’s extravagance, the clothes had either been sold or pawned. The once-stuffed trunk was empty. Unexpectedly, the blood drip to the floor below exposed the crime. Although she recounted her tragic experiences in detail during interrogation, she still failed to win trust.
So in a subsequent interrogation, she changed her statement and admitted someone had helped her commit the crime. He was named Xia Xianhui, nicknamed Xia “Big Devil,” a neighbor who often helped her.
Seeing her plight, he had repeatedly given her money. Gradually, the two became close, even crossing the boundary of mere neighbors. Mrs. Zhang vaguely said perhaps she had repaid his kindness—or perhaps feelings had developed.
She and Xia had had illicit relations many times. The police immediately investigated and found that the day after the crime, Xia left Shanghai for elsewhere, which aroused suspicion. The police did everything they could to find him.
Xia admitted to an illicit relationship with Mrs. Zhang but resolutely denied taking part in the killing. He frankly said he had no real feelings for her and was just fooling around with the circumstances. Before the police could clarify, Mrs. Zhang changed her story again, naming another man: Xia Baoyu.
Nicknamed “Little Lingbo,” he was a friend of Zhang Wenying. Mrs. Zhang claimed that two days before the crime she had discussed a plan to kill with Xia Baoyu and had prepared many things. On the day of the murder, taking advantage of her husband’s deep sleep, she opened the door for Xia and handed him a vegetable knife.
She then watched Xia slash her husband’s neck. After he died, she, the “venomous wife,” added one more stroke to his forehead. This testimony threw the police into confusion, and they sought out Xia Baoyu.
But Xia Baoyu, frightened and flustered, could not explain himself clearly and vigorously denied involvement. A week later, the case took yet another turn: Mrs. Zhang again revised her statement, saying neither of the men she had mentioned had anything to do with it.
She said she had named Xia Xianhui because she had been subjected to prolonged interrogation and coercion, leaving her mind unclear so she lashed out impulsively. As for blaming Xia Baoyu, it was because she hated him: he had repeatedly encouraged her husband to gamble.
In her heart, her husband had been ruined by Xia Baoyu. After investigation, both men had alibis at the time of the crime, and suspicion was ruled out. More than a month later, in May 1945, the case was brought to trial.
Mrs. Zhang was charged with murder. In court she wore a gray cheongsam. Thin and wan, her face looked delicate, yet she was properly dressed. She kept a calm expression and recounted the details of the crime.

To each question from the judge, she gave systematic answers. The court held that she had not tried to flee after the crime and that her courtroom statements were logically coherent.
This showed she was fully conscious when she offended and fully responsible criminally. In the end, the court sentenced Mrs. Zhang to death, giving sixteen characters as the reason: “Self-defense failed; circumstances intolerable; dismembered body; abominable beyond measure.” The Wangs, for reporting the case promptly, received a reward.
Before execution, Mrs. Zhang was held at Tielan Prison in Shanghai. Public opinion was divided. Some, firm in the notion of “a life for a life,” strongly approved the verdict.
They saw Mrs. Zhang as a femme fatale of the modern age who deserved her fate. Others sympathized with her, seeing her as a victim of domestic violence. Killing her husband, they said, was a desperate act by someone driven into a corner.
They believed the death penalty was too harsh. Among those who held this view were writers and intellectuals who published articles to defend Mrs. Zhang.
Among them was Su Cheng, one of the four great talented women of the literary world at the time. In her article defending the husband-killer, she left passionate words: “Only those who endure abuse day after day will grow cruel.”
“If a man who sheds blood is a hero, must a woman who sheds blood be a criminal?” Su Cheng even candidly stated the reason Mrs. Zhang killed her husband: “She tried to die but could not.”
“So she had no choice but to kill.” After the article was published, Su Cheng immediately became the focus of public opinion. Some even used this to attack her.
They said she was as unchaste as Mrs. Zhang, thus her defense. Nevertheless, Su Cheng stood by her view and published several more pieces to clarify it.
The poet Guan Lu also wrote sharply worded essays, rebutting, “Both the cruel husband and the adulteress are hateful. Why then can’t the hateful man be killed?” Sympathy snowballed, drawing more attention to the suffering Mrs. Zhang had endured. Under powerful public pressure, her execution was postponed repeatedly. Some lawyers volunteered to defend her pro bono, seeking a sliver of hope for her life.
Hearing the understanding voices, Mrs. Zhang, who had been resigned to death, changed her attitude and began appealing. She behaved well in prison; when assigned to the embroidery team, her innate dexterity resulted in high-quality, fast work, making her a model inmate. The appearance of a key figure further delayed her execution.
This person was an elderly Catholic nun who testified at the appellate hearing, saying that Mrs. Zhang was pregnant and the baby was innocent; even if she were to be executed, it must be after childbirth. In fact, the pregnancy was untrue.
But given the medical conditions of the time, pregnancy could not be accurately verified, so the court had to follow her statement and postpone the execution. Unexpectedly, before she “gave birth” in August 1945, Japan announced unconditional surrender. With the disbanding and restructuring of judicial organs, previous rulings were deemed invalid.
In August 1946, a year and a half after the crime, the Shanghai High Court retried the case. Mrs. Zhang wore a blue shirt and looked particularly haggard. Her short hair at shoulder length highlighted the melancholy between her brows. She revealed more details, saying that on the day of the crime, she had gone to use the toilet and happened to see a knife in a drawer.
In that instant, she felt possessed, picked up the knife, and acted. The knife, originally bought to start her pork-chop-and-fries stall, unexpectedly became the tool of death. She emphasized the crime had not been premeditated but was the result of a moment’s impulse.

Her account was clearer than at the first trial, and statements favorable to her had increased, likely thanks to her lawyer’s guidance. Asked whether Zhang made any sounds at the time of the attack, she thought for a long time before answering in the negative. She claimed she was unconscious at the time and did not even realize she had accidentally injured her own finger. Her defense counsel pleaded for mercy, focusing on her tragic circumstances.
Even more surprising, the lawyer made a shocking claim: Zhang Wenying was suspected of collaboration with the Japanese; thus, Mrs. Zhang’s act might even be considered meritorious. The courtroom burst into laughter.
Whether that was true can no longer be verified. But the argument showed the lawyer’s effort to mitigate her sentence. After a month of hearings, in September the court again found her guilty and sentenced her to death.
She appealed. Despite her dire situation, Mrs. Zhang continued to attract attention from all sectors. Reporters occasionally sought news of her in prison and published small tidbits.
These were mostly trivial details that nevertheless aroused public curiosity—for example, that she had gained weight, was in good health, got along with other inmates, and behaved appropriately. Society continued to speak up for her. Writers’ vivid prose and lawyers’ eloquence formed a sturdy protective net around her.
Their efforts were not in vain. Nearly three years after the crime, Mrs. Zhang’s sentence was commuted to 15 years’ imprisonment. Upon hearing the result, she was overjoyed, as if granted a new life.
In 1949, she was transferred to a Shanghai farm in Dafeng, Jiangsu, to serve her sentence. There, she had a chance to apply for release under supervision.
To regain freedom, she wrote to Wang Tayang, begging him to act as guarantor. But after sending the letter and hearing nothing for a long time, she later learned that it wasn’t that he was unwilling, but that he dared not agree. She had to give up. After completing her sentence, she settled in Dafeng.
At that time, she was completely alone—no relatives, no friends, no fixed abode. Arranged by the authorities, she went to work at a Turkish-owned company. There, she met her future husband, Yan Shaohua, two years her junior.
He knew her past clearly but did not mind. They married in 1959 and walked the rest of life together. Mrs. Zhang cherished this hard-won marriage deeply.
She was 46 when she married. Happiness, though late, finally arrived. This case, lacking photo records and preserved mostly in text, gave rise to many rumors.
Among them was the rumor that she had been hanged in prison as a warning, leading the public to think she had long been dead. To clarify the truth, in 1990, reporter Tu Bing of the Shanghai-based newspaper “Shanghai Bund” conducted an investigation. Guided by locals to a private house in Dafeng, he knocked for a long time without an answer.
Just as he was about to peer through the window, a slightly hoarse voice startled him: “Who are you looking for?” He turned to see a small but spry elderly woman. He recognized her immediately—it was Mrs. Zhang, or more precisely, Chu Huizhen.
After her release, she had changed her name. That day, she still retained the simplicity of bygone days. Time had carved fine wrinkles into her face.

Although she was 74, her eyes still shone with resilience and sometimes revealed a certain steely decisiveness. Tu Bing surmised this was closely related to the half a lifetime of suffering she had endured.
Chu Huizhen opened the door and accepted the interview. Her home was simple. She said that apart from the house itself, she had purchased all the furnishings herself.
At this, she chuckled and said, “It’s not bad. Now I have more than 100 yuan a month in pension.” At the time, that amount was enough for a modest, worry-free life.
Before her crime, Chu Huizhen had no children. Prison took away her chance to be a mother. Though childless, she had deep affection for children.
She loved her work at the Turkish company and stayed until retirement. Her home was filled with children’s photos and drawings. In her free years, wherever she went, she would take in a few children as goddaughters and godsons to fill the deep void in her heart.
While she was talking with Tu Bing, a little girl came over. Holding and playing with the child, she hummed cheerful nursery rhymes. Each wrinkle on her face seemed soothed by happiness. Seeing this kindly old woman so full of innocence and contentment, Tu Bing could not help but sigh.
Who could connect this gentle, kind grandmother with the woman once labeled a venomous wife who killed her husband in her youth? Chu Huizhen’s husband had died of illness a few years earlier. Speaking of him, a gentle expression appeared on her face. She said what she cherished most was their wedding photo under glass on the bedside cabinet.
However, when the 1945 case was mentioned again, she fell silent for a long time. Clearly, she did not want to revisit that painful, indelible period. She only said softly, “I want to live to old age and treat myself well.”
“Killing was my last battle for survival. No one protected me; only I could save myself.” Beyond that, she said no more.
After the interview, reporter Tu Bing hurried to the crime scene. After the case, the house had remained unoccupied. Later, a bolder private detective moved in. There, Tu met an elderly woman on the third floor, surnamed Zhang.
Mrs. Zhang was delighted to see a reporter and proactively cooperated. She revealed a surprising detail: the first witness was not actually Wang Tayang—it was her.
Early that morning, when she went downstairs to fetch water, she had accidentally touched the blood on the stair rail and sensed something was wrong. She told Wang and showed him. Only then did Wang “discover” it and call the police. After the interview, Tu took the same route back and encountered some locals.

When the case was mentioned, their calm faces suddenly lit up in surprise. It turned out that in the decades since her release, Chu had never returned to Shanghai, nor deliberately learned about changes in Jiangyuan Alley. Because they had not seen her for years, people were curious about her current situation.
So Tu took out recent photos of Chu to show them, eliciting a chorus of sighs. Over time, Xin Xue Road and Jiangyuan Alley had long since been demolished. Now it was a residential area with high-rise buildings. The shabby appearance of the old alley was gone—only history remained.
The impact of this old Shanghai case has not faded with time. Taiwanese writer Li Ang used it as the prototype for her novel “The Butcher’s Wife.” The novel not only won awards but was translated into many languages and drew international attention.
Later, The Butcher’s Wife was adapted into films and television dramas. In 2020, the film “Jiangyuan Road Cold Case,” adapted from this case, was slated for release, bringing the sensational case back into public view. One filming location, “The Butcher’s Street,” became a popular check-in spot, crowded with visitors taking photos.
Chu Huizhen transformed her despair into resilient strength, yet she could not escape the shackles of feudal propriety. Her story exposes the misery and struggle of women in the old patriarchal society and the bondage of traditional notions. Fortunately, social progress eventually lifted a heavy knife from her fate.
Though decades have passed, her story still echoes like a whisper from the past, reminding us of the true meaning of justice and compassion, and that sometimes an individual’s survival is a mirror reflecting a society slowly awakening. And in any case, in our present era, taking a life as she did is entirely wrong. That’s the end of today’s video—see you next time.
